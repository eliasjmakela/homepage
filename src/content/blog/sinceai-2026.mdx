Last weekend, I got the chance to travel to Turku and participate in my very first hackathon, SinceAI 2026, with a couple of friends.
Though we didn't win any challenges this time, I thought it was a really insightful experience.
Interestingly, it wasn't really about technology, but business. It was a lot like what I imagine consulting being:
working with clients to understand their problems, breaking it down and really trying to grasp the dynamics and players involved,
and then from there, trying to build a novel way of fixing things.

After building that understanding, it really became about teamwork.
Of course in today's age, we had to use a very agentic workflow,
but the useful part about that might've actually been seeing where that broke down.
We all had our own agents working on parts of the project, but there was inevitably some overlap,
and the miscommunications about what we were actually building just sort of multiplied in pace with how fast we were doing it.
I guess that's the thing about AI in a project like this:
agents can help you really put the pedal to the metal, but it's up to you to know where you're going if you don't want to crash.

In the future, I think it might be interesting to work on ways to have agents communicate together more efficiently.
There, too, I don't think the technical implementation is the hard part. There are a number of ways of setting up channels
where different agents could coordinate work over some sort of network in a shared message format. The complex part is understanding
the working dynamics of agents. How do you get them to really "agree"? They might pass over specs and tasks to each other,
but I find those can become bloated quickly and over time, the agents start losing track of what's important.

Maybe one avenue to solve this would be a hierarchy of some kind. A master orchestrator agent who always has the specifications
and most important knowledge in context, and makes sure every agent keeps its eye on the ball. But this fails by violating the
subsidiarity principle: as agents work on whatever small part of the whole, they will surely discover new things and better
approaches to the problems that the orchestrator agent with a birds-eye view couldn't have known, and forcing the subagent to obey orders
from somebody who knows less than they do doesn't make sense (as any person who's ever had a bad boss will be able to tell you).

Perhaps this could be solved with some kind of information sharing / learning system. As the lower-level agents work on their
own tasks, they might let the orchestrator agent know about insights or discoveries they've made, which the orchestrator can
then use to further improve the way they organize everything. This seems promising, but team dynamics and the way knowledge
is passed between actors (be they human or AI) is in my opinion, genuinely complex and kind of hard to wrap one's head around
just in theory. The only way to see how this could work for real, though, is
probably just to try to do it and see what gets screwy first. Next weekend I will be participating in Junction's main event, so
I might just get a chance to try it out then.
